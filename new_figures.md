Figure 1. Nighttime storm-relative sea-surface temperature (SST) cold wake associated with LALA and Lowell across the central and eastern North Pacific. Blue shading shows pixels that experienced a peak nighttime SST cooling of at least 0.5 °C relative to their pixel-specific pre-storm baseline. Peak cooling was calculated as the difference between the median nighttime SST during the −120 to −48 h pre-passage baseline period and the minimum nighttime SST observed during the 0 to +120 h period following the closest storm passage; therefore, larger positive values indicate stronger post-storm cooling. The two storm tracks were treated separately rather than concatenated, preventing the creation of an artificial track segment between the end of LALA and the beginning of Lowell. ## Result
The combined LALA–Lowell analysis reveals a spatially heterogeneous but coherent storm-related cold-wake response across the study domain. Cooling exceeding the 0.5 °C threshold is concentrated primarily along and adjacent to portions of the two cyclone tracks rather than being uniformly distributed throughout the North Pacific. The strongest cooling appears in several localized regions where the storm tracks and stronger wind footprints overlap, indicating that the SST response was highly spatially variable.
One of the most pronounced cold-wake signals occurs near approximately 14–15°N, 164–166°W, where cooling reaches roughly 1.8–1.9 °C based on the plotted color scale. This cooling maximum occurs close to a section of the storm track characterized by hurricane-strength conditions and within the nested 34-, 50-, and 64-kt wind-swath footprints. A second prominent cooling region occurs farther northwest, approximately near 29–31°N, 169–172°W, where substantial cooling is also evident near the cyclone track and associated wind field. These geographically separated maxima demonstrate that the largest SST response does not occur continuously along the full storm trajectory but instead develops in distinct regions.
The spatial correspondence between stronger cooling and the wind-swath footprints is especially notable. Areas enclosed by the higher-wind contours, particularly the 50- and 64-kt footprints, frequently coincide with enhanced cooling. In contrast, much of the peripheral 34-kt wind footprint exhibits weaker or more spatially fragmented SST responses. This pattern is consistent with stronger storm forcing contributing to a larger ocean surface response, although the figure alone does not establish wind speed as the sole control on cooling. Other factors—including storm translation speed, upper-ocean stratification, mixed-layer depth, pre-storm thermal structure, storm asymmetry, and the duration of wind forcing—could also contribute to the observed spatial variability.
The map also shows that substantial cooling can extend beyond the immediate storm centerline. In several locations, the cold wake forms a relatively broad band surrounding or trailing the cyclone track, suggesting that the oceanic response reflects the integrated wind forcing over an extended storm footprint rather than only conditions directly beneath the storm center. This is particularly apparent around the major cooling region near ~14°N, where the strongest cooling occupies a broad area adjacent to the track.
An important methodological feature of this analysis is that the timing of the SST response is determined independently for every pixel. Each pixel is assigned to either LALA or Lowell according to which storm passes geographically closer, and its baseline and post-storm SST windows are then calculated relative to that storm's local closest-passage time.    Pasted markdown This is preferable to using a single basin-wide storm date because it accounts for the large spatial and temporal progression of the storms across the domain. The pre-storm reference is based on the median nighttime SST from −120 to −48 h, while peak cooling is based on the minimum SST during the following 120 h.    Pasted markdown Consequently, the map represents the maximum detectable cold-wake magnitude during the first five days following local storm passage, rather than SST conditions at one fixed post-storm time.

Figure 2.Spearman rank correlation between antecedent storm forcing and nighttime SST cooling for LALA and Lowell across non-overlapping 12-h forcing intervals preceding local storm passage.

Nighttime SST responses were calculated relative to a pixel-specific pre-storm baseline defined as the median SST from $-120$ to $-48\text{h}$ before local closest passage. Peak cooling was defined as the baseline SST minus the minimum nighttime SST observed from $0$ to $+120\text{h}$ after passage, whereas sustained cooling was defined as the baseline SST minus the median nighttime SST from $+24$ to $+72\text{h}$.

Storm forcing was quantified for each antecedent interval using the integrated wind-intensity metric:

$$\sum (V_{\text{max}} - 34\text{kt})^2 \times 1\text{h}$$

with wind speeds below $34\,\text{kt}$ contributing zero forcing. Positive lag labels denote hours before local passage; thus, 0–12 h prior corresponds to $-12$ to $0\text{h}$ in storm-relative time.

Cooling responses were aggregated into 50-km along-track bins. Peak-cooling statistics were evaluated within the observed NHC $\ge 34\text{kt}$ wind footprint, whereas sustained-cooling statistics were evaluated within the $\ge 34\text{-kt}$ footprint expanded by a $2^\circ$ buffer.

Shaded envelopes indicate 95% confidence intervals estimated using a 250-km spatial block bootstrap with 2,000 bootstrap realizations. The horizontal dotted line indicates zero correlation.

## Result
Spearman rank correlations revealed distinct storm-specific relationships between antecedent wind forcing and nighttime SST cooling. For LALA, both peak and sustained cooling showed weak relationships with forcing immediately before local passage, followed by a pronounced increase in correlation at intermediate antecedent times. Peak cooling reached its maximum correlation during the 48–60 h prior interval ($$\rho=0.557\$$, 95% CI: 0.187–0.784), while sustained cooling reached its strongest association during 36–48 h prior ($$\rho=0.689\$$, 95% CI: 0.479–0.813). Correlations subsequently weakened at longer antecedent intervals. This pattern indicates that the spatial variability in LALA-induced cooling was most strongly associated with storm forcing occurring approximately 1.5–2.5 days before local passage, rather than with forcing immediately preceding passage.

For Lowell, peak cooling exhibited a broadly similar but weaker intermediate-lag response. The maximum Spearman correlation occurred during 36–48 h prior ($$\rho=0.538\$$, 95% CI: 0.203–0.728), with a comparably strong relationship during 48–60 h prior ($$\rho=0.531\$$, 95% CI: 0.150–0.780). In contrast, Lowell sustained cooling showed its strongest association much closer to local passage, peaking during 12–24 h prior ($$\rho=0.449\$$, 95% CI: 0.034–0.743). The sustained-cooling correlation then declined progressively with increasing lead time and approached zero or became negative at the longest antecedent intervals.

Taken together, these patterns show that peak SST cooling in both storms was most strongly associated with antecedent forcing occurring approximately 36–60 h before local passage, whereas the timing of the sustained response differed between LALA and Lowell. LALA sustained cooling remained most strongly associated with forcing 36–48 h before passage, while Lowell sustained cooling was more closely related to forcing during the final 24 h before passage. This contrast highlights substantial storm-to-storm variability in the timing between antecedent wind forcing and the subsequent nighttime SST response.

Figure 3. Storm-relative peak chlorophyll-a enhancement associated with LALA and Lowell across the central and eastern North Pacific.

Chlorophyll-a enhancement was calculated relative to a pixel-specific pre-storm baseline defined over $-120$ to $-48\text{h}$ before local storm passage, with the post-storm response defined as the maximum chlorophyll-a concentration from $0$ to $+120\text{h}$ after passage. Only positive enhancements of at least $0.03\text{mg}\ \text{m}^{-3}$ are displayed. For locations influenced by both storms, the mapped response represents the pixelwise maximum positive enhancement from either LALA or Lowell, rather than the sum of the two responses. Chlorophyll-a enhancement is shown on a logarithmic color scale, with warmer colors indicating larger increases. Storm tracks are colored by intensity category, white-centered markers indicate best-track positions, black outlines denote coral reefs, and the 34-, 50-, and 64-kt wind-swath boundaries delineate the corresponding storm wind footprints. The merged field is expressed in $\text{mg}\ \text{m}^{-3}$, and the map preserves the larger response where valid LALA and Lowell enhancement fields overlap.

## Result
Storm-relative chlorophyll-a enhancement was spatially heterogeneous for both LALA and Lowell (Fig. 3), with the strongest increases generally concentrated along or near portions of the storm tracks enclosed by the NHC wind-swath footprints. For LALA, enhanced chlorophyll-a was most apparent along the recurving central Pacific portion of the track, particularly between approximately 20 and 30°N, whereas Lowell produced a pronounced response along its lower-latitude track near 13–15°N. In both cases, enhancement occurred in spatially discrete patches rather than as a continuous response throughout the wind footprint. Several of the largest increases coincided with hurricane-strength sections of the storm tracks and with the 50- and 64-kt wind-swath regions, although substantial spatial variability remained. Because peak enhancement was defined as the maximum chlorophyll-a increase during the first 120 h following local passage relative to a −120 to −48 h pre-storm baseline, the mapped distribution represents the largest post-storm biological response observed within the five-day response window.

Figure 4. Spearman rank correlation between antecedent storm forcing and storm-relative chlorophyll-a enhancement for LALA and Lowell across non-overlapping 12-h forcing intervals preceding local storm passage.

Chlorophyll-a responses were calculated independently for each storm using NOAA MSL12 DINEOF 2-km daily chlorophyll-a observations. For each pixel, the pre-storm baseline was defined as the median chlorophyll-a concentration from $-120$ to $-48\text{h}$ relative to local closest passage. Peak enhancement was calculated as the maximum chlorophyll-a concentration from $0$ to $+120\,\text{h}$ minus the baseline, whereas sustained enhancement was calculated as the median chlorophyll-a concentration from $+24$ to $+72\text{h}$ minus the baseline; only positive chlorophyll-a changes were retained.

Responses were summarized in 50-km along-track bins within 50 km of the corresponding storm track. Storm intensity was interpolated to hourly resolution, and antecedent forcing for each 12-h interval was quantified from wind speeds exceeding $34\text{kt}$ using:

$$\sum (V_{\text{max}} - 34\text{kt})^2 \times 1\text{h}$$

Lines show Spearman’s rank correlation coefficient ($\rho$) for peak and sustained chlorophyll-a enhancement, while shaded regions indicate 95% confidence intervals derived from a 250-km spatial block bootstrap with 2,000 realizations. The horizontal dotted line denotes zero correlation.

## Result
The timing of the chlorophyll-a response differed substantially between LALA and Lowell and between peak and sustained enhancement metrics. For LALA, peak enhancement was most strongly associated with forcing 72–84 h prior to local passage, while sustained enhancement exhibited its strongest relationship at 108–120 h prior. In contrast, Lowell peak enhancement was most strongly related to forcing during the final 0–12 h before passage, whereas its sustained response peaked at 60–72 h prior. These contrasting lag structures indicate that the biological response was not uniformly linked to forcing at the time of closest storm passage and instead varied with both storm and response timescale.
